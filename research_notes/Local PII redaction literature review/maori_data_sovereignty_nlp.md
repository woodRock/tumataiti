## 1. Māori data sovereignty: principles, governance models and the NZ legal context

### Takeaway
Māori data sovereignty is now well codified. It rests on Te Mana Raraunga's 2018 principles, the CARE principles (2020), Te Kāhui Raraunga's Māori Data Governance Model (2023), Crown–iwi agreements such as Mana Ōrite (2019), and Kukutai and Taylor's 2016 founding volume. The common thread is **collective** rights over data. That matters for Tūmataiti: iwi/hapū affiliation and whakapapa are collective, tapu data, not just individual PII. Classifying data as tapu (controlled) or noa (open) is explicitly something for Māori to decide.

### Cited Findings

**Foundational texts**
- **Kukutai, T. & Taylor, J. (eds.) (2016). *Indigenous Data Sovereignty: Toward an Agenda*. CAEPR Research Monograph No. 38. Canberra: ANU Press. Open access.** The edited volume takes UNDRIP as its premise and argues that Indigenous peoples have inherent and inalienable rights over the collection, ownership and application of data about them, their lifeways and their territories. It grew out of a 2015 Canberra workshop and is described as the first book on the topic written from an Indigenous standpoint. It focuses on the CANZUS states. Relevance: this is the foundational citation for treating Māori data as subject to collective rights. — [ANU Open Research](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/items/210eb2f2-de8e-4788-946b-09b257d51d7c); [ANU CIPR listing](https://cipr.cass.anu.edu.au/research/publications/indigenous-data-sovereignty-toward-agenda); [Royal Society Te Apārangi 150 list](https://www.royalsociety.org.nz/150th-anniversary/tetakarangi/indigenous-data-sovereignty-toward-an-agendajohn-taylor-and-tahu-kukutai-eds-2016). The CAEPR38 series tag appears in catalogue records; I did not see the monograph number on the ANU page itself. **[not fetched]**
- **Te Mana Raraunga (2018). *Principles of Māori Data Sovereignty* (brief, October 2018).** The principles are Rangatiratanga (authority: control, jurisdiction, self-determination), Whakapapa (relationships), Whanaungatanga (obligations), Kotahitanga (collective benefit), Manaakitanga (reciprocity) and Kaitiakitanga (guardianship). Two provisions bear directly on this project: "Māori shall decide which Māori data shall be controlled (tapu) or open (noa) access", and the warning that analysis which stigmatises or blames Māori causes collective and individual harm. — [TMR principles page](https://www.temanararaunga.maori.nz/principles-of-maori-data-sovereignty); [PDF mirror (Otago Polytechnic)](https://online.op.ac.nz/assets/LearningTeachingDevelopment/TMR+Maori+Data+Sovereignty+Principles+Oct+2018.pdf); adopted, for example, by the [Tertiary Education Commission](https://www.tec.govt.nz/teo/working-with-teos/analysing-student-data/key-components/community-perspectives/maori-data-sovereignty). **[not fetched; content from search summaries]**
- **Carroll, S. R., Garba, I., Figueroa-Rodríguez, O. L., Holbrook, J., Lovett, R., Materechera, S., Parsons, M., Raseroka, K., Rodriguez-Lonebear, D., Rowe, R., Sara, R., Walker, J. D., Anderson, J. & Hudson, M. (2020). The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. *Data Science Journal*, 19(1), 43. DOI: 10.5334/dsj-2020-043.** The paper sets out Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility and Ethics as a people- and purpose-oriented complement to FAIR. Māui Hudson (Waikato), a founding member of Te Mana Raraunga, is a co-author. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5334/dsj-2020-043); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/b3fcded6f18a4d0488c20ce01be01994); [Ocean Best Practices record](https://repository.oceanbestpractices.org/handle/11329/1507). The full author list is from my knowledge, and the search confirmed "Carroll and 13 co-authors". Check the list against the DOI landing page before citing.
- **Kukutai, T. (2024). How Indigenous communities in New Zealand are protecting their data. *Science*, 384(6691), 4 April 2024. DOI: 10.1126/science.ado9298.** (I extracted the text from the Waikato-hosted PDF.) Kukutai argues that individual data rights are limited because tech firms can "link, surveil, target, and make inferences about groups", and that Indigenous data sovereignty is mainly about collective rights. The paper also reports that:
  - Māori are about 17% of the population, and most record at least one iwi affiliation in the census.
  - The 2018 Census missed nearly one-third of Māori.
  - Te Hiku Media has issued Kaitiakitanga Licences for its Rongo app, its Whare Kōrero app and the **Papa Reo API**. The API licence prohibits surveillance, tracking, building Māori corpora, mining Māori data, and anything inconsistent with Māori data sovereignty principles. Access requires a legally binding agreement.
  - Whakapapa data, which includes ancestry, DNA and connections to mountains and rivers, is "widely considered to be tapu".
  - Āhau's decentralised whakapapa platform keeps data on whānau-controlled "pātaka" storehouses.
  - Te Kāhui Raraunga was established in 2019.
  — [Waikato PDF](https://www.waikato.ac.nz/assets/Uploads/Research/Research-institutes-centres-and-groups/Institutes/TKRI/Publications/Science_Kukutai2024_How_Indigenous_Communities_Protecting_Data.pdf). This source is directly relevant to the claim that whakapapa and affiliation are tapu categories.

**Governance models and Crown–iwi arrangements**
- **Kukutai, T., Campbell-Kamariera, K., Mead, A., Mikaere, K., Moses, C., Whitehead, J. & Cormack, D. (2023). *Māori Data Governance Model*. Te Kāhui Raraunga, May 2023.** This is system-wide guidance for governing Māori data in the NZ public service, consistent with Te Tiriti obligations, and it supports iwi and hapū sovereignty over their data. It is organised into pou. The Digital Public Service cites Pou 4 (data protection) and Pou 8 (data classification) as key for agencies. Researchers have since operationalised it, for example as a 24-criterion audit tool in health. — [Waikato Research Commons](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/items/c076bc80-973b-4389-ac04-3902de003d48); [TMR resource hub](https://temanararaunga.maori.nz/resource-hub-copy); [DNS Māori data guidance](https://dns.govt.nz/standards-and-guidance/information-sharing-standard/maori-data); [data.govt.nz blog](https://data.govt.nz/blog/maori-data-governance-and-maori-data-aspirations). **[not fetched; I did not read the primary PDF]**
- **Mana Ōrite Relationship Agreement (Stats NZ and the Data Iwi Leaders Group), signed 30 October 2019.** Stats NZ's cover note calls it the first agreement of its kind between iwi-Māori and the Crown. It underpins a shared work programme, including Te Whata (an iwi data storehouse) and co-designed methods for recording iwi affiliation in the 2023 Census. The Data ILG operates as Te Kāhui Raraunga Charitable Trust. A **separate** Mana Ōrite agreement with the Department of Internal Affairs was signed on 22 June 2021. The cover note asks anyone quoting the agreement to contact the parties first, because it contains mātauranga Māori. — [Stats NZ PDF](https://www.stats.govt.nz/assets/Uploads/mana-orite-relationship-agreement-with-cover-note-12-february-2021.pdf); [Digital Government page](https://digital.govt.nz/digital-government/leadership/mana-orite-relationship-agreement)

**Māori perspectives on AI specifically**
- **Taiuru, K. (2020). *Te Tiriti o Waitangi and Māori Ethics Guidelines for AI, Algorithms, Data and IoT*. CC BY 4.0.** The guidelines call for AI to be co-designed, co-initiated and co-governed with Māori and iwi, and for compliance with tikanga and transparency. Taiuru recommends that organisations define Māori data, engage Māori stakeholders on tapu and noa, and label Māori data. — [taiuru.maori.nz/tiritiethicalguide](https://www.taiuru.maori.nz/tiritiethicalguide); [ACE Aotearoa summary](https://www.aceaotearoa.org.nz/node/1005)
- **Taiuru, K. — *He Tangata, He Karetao, He Ātārangi*: an Indigenous Peoples AI framework (reported March 2026).** It helps iwi and Māori organisations judge whether an AI system respects cultural values and protects Māori data. — [Waatea News, 11 March 2026](https://waateanews.com/2026/03/11/tech-indigenous-ai-framework-offers-path-for-maori-in-the-age-of-artificial-intelligence/) **[not fetched]**
- **Taiuru & Maclaurin: Māori principles for predictive and generative AI in healthcare.** The principles include mana whakahaere (stewardship/kaitiakitanga of Māori health data) and mana motuhake (self-determination). They are listed by the Office of the Prime Minister's Chief Science Advisor. — [PMCSA](https://www.pmcsa.ac.nz/?p=13083) **[not fetched]**
- A Victoria University of Wellington ECS research group talk, "Māori Data and AI", exists as a PDF. — [ecs.wgtn.ac.nz PDF](https://ecs.wgtn.ac.nz/foswiki/pub/Groups/ECRG/Talks/MāoriDataAndAI.pdf) **[not fetched; author not confirmed]**

### Inferences
- The frameworks all locate harm at the **collective** level: TMR on stigmatising analysis, Kukutai (2024) on group inference, and CARE's "Authority to control". So Tūmataiti should treat iwi, hapū, marae and whakapapa mentions as a sensitive category in their own right, redacted by default, even when no personal name appears.
- TMR says Māori decide what is tapu and what is noa. That suggests the taxonomy and default policy for culturally sensitive categories should be governed by Māori advisors, not set by developers alone. CARE's "Authority to control" points the same way.
- The Papa Reo API licence prohibits "building Māori corpora" and "mining Māori data". Any te reo evaluation or training data drawn from Te Hiku resources would need explicit agreement, and synthetic name lexicons should avoid scraping Kaitiakitanga-licensed content.

### Gaps
- I did not read the full text of the Māori Data Governance Model, so I could not list all its pou.
- I found no NZ government AI-specific guidance on Māori data published in 2025–26 beyond the Biometrics Code. I did not search for the Public Service AI Framework or the Algorithm Charter, and they should be checked.
- I did not locate a peer-reviewed Māori-authored paper on AI by Māui Hudson, or by "Brown" as named in the brief. The search surfaced Petera Hudson (Massey), who has published on Māori knowledge and AI in *JRSNZ* and *IEEE Annals*, but I found no specific titles or DOIs. — [St Paul's Collegiate profile](https://community.stpauls.school.nz/news/collegians/189/189-Dr-Petera-Hudson-Leading-Maaori-AI-research)

## 2. Te reo Māori NLP: ASR, corpora, code-switching, loanwords, macrons, NER and tokenisation

### Takeaway
Te reo language technology is led by Te Hiku Media/Papa Reo for ASR and by Waikato and Auckland researchers for code-switching, loanword corpora and macron restoration. The resources are small, often licensed under Kaitiakitanga terms, and **I found no published te reo Māori NER dataset or system**. Tūmataiti's te reo name evaluation would therefore be among the first of its kind. Macron restoration is a largely solved word-level task (over 95% accuracy). Some words share spelling across Māori and English, which defeats rule-based language identification.

### Cited Findings

**ASR and Te Hiku Media / Papa Reo**
- Te Hiku Media reports that its te reo ASR achieves **92% accuracy for te reo Māori and 82% for bilingual speech**. It is built with NVIDIA NeMo and trained only on material contributed with consent. — [NVIDIA blog](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/te-hiku-media-maori-speech-ai/); [ITU, 2022](https://www.itu.int/hub/2022/08/ai-indigenous-languages-maori-te-reo/). These figures are vendor-reported and not peer-reviewed.
- **Mahelona, K., Leoni, G., Duncan, S. & Thompson, M. (2023). "OpenAI's Whisper is another case study in Colonisation." Papa Reo blog.** The post argues that Whisper was trained on 1,381 hours of te reo collected without Māori consent, out of 680,000 hours in total. It holds that Māori language tools should be built by Māori-led organisations. Sources date it to February or March 2023. — [SSRC Just Tech repost](https://just-tech.ssrc.org/2023/03/openais-whisper-is-another-case-study-in-colonisation-papa-reo/?pdf=7809); [Simon Willison link post, 8 Feb 2023](https://simonwillison.net/2023/Feb/8/whisper-colonisation/)
- **Te Hiku Media (2022, updated 2023). "Data sovereignty and the Kaitiakitanga License."** The licence is hosted on Te Hiku's GitHub. Te Hiku describes itself as kaitiaki of the data, not its owner, prioritises requests from Indigenous revitalisation groups, and frames Big Tech as a threat. — [tehiku.nz](https://tehiku.nz/te-hiku-tech/te-hiku-dev-korero/25141/data-sovereignty-and-the-kaitiakitanga-license); see also [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/indigenous-ai-voice-models-maori) and [MIT Technology Review, "Artificial intelligence for the people" (2022)](https://www.technologyreview.com/2022/04/22/1050394/artificial-intelligence-for-the-people/) **[not fetched]**
- **Coto-Solano, R., et al. (2022). Development of Automatic Speech Recognition for the Documentation of Cook Islands Māori. *Proceedings of LREC 2022*, pp. 3872–3882. ACL Anthology 2022.lrec-1.412.** The study used four hours of speech in a language with about 22,000 speakers. Wav2Vec2 matched Kaldi on CER (6±1) but did worse on WER (23 against 18). Performance on unseen speakers fell to CER 15 and WER 46. The model is released under a licence based on the Kaitiakitanga Licence. Relevance: a Pasifika/Polynesian comparator, and an example of the Kaitiakitanga licence being adopted beyond Te Hiku. — [ACL Anthology](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2022.lrec-1.412)

**Corpora and code-switching**
- **James, J., Shields, I., Yogarajan, V., Keegan, P. J., Watson, C., Jones, P.-L. & Mahelona, K. (2022). The Development of a Labelled te reo Māori–English Bilingual Database for Language Technology. arXiv:2208.09778** (submitted to *Language Resources and Evaluation*). The database contains **66,016,807 words** of NZ Parliament Hansard with word-level language labels, produced by rules plus expert annotation. Some words are spelled identically in Māori and English with different meanings, so rule-based labelling could not handle them. The paper lists the other available Māori text resources: the Reo Māori Twitter corpus, the Legal Māori Corpus, and a Te Hiku Hansard corpus whose labels were not hand-corrected. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09778)
- **James, J., Yogarajan, V., Shields, I., Watson, C., Keegan, P., Mahelona, K. & Jones, P.-L. (2022). Language Models for Code-switch Detection of te reo Māori and English in a Low-resource Setting. *Findings of NAACL 2022*. ACL Anthology 2022.findings-naacl.49.** A BiLSTM with bilingual sub-word embeddings reached **87% accuracy** at detecting code-switch points. The authors report that cloud systems such as Google and Azure are inaccurate at detecting Māori. — [ACL Anthology](https://preview.aclanthology.org/moar-dois/2022.findings-naacl.49); [author page](https://aclanthology.org/people/vithya-yogarajan/unverified/)
- **Trye, D., Yogarajan, V., König, J., Keegan, T. T., Bainbridge, D. & Apperley, M. (2022). A Hybrid Architecture for Labelling Bilingual Māori–English Tweets. *Findings of AACL-IJCNLP 2022*. ACL Anthology 2022.findings-aacl.11.** The hybrid word-level labeller was used to annotate a new bilingual Twitter corpus, which is available on request. — [author page](https://aclanthology.org/people/vithya-yogarajan/unverified/)
- **Trye, D., Calude, A. S., Bravo-Marquez, F. & Keegan, T. T. (2019). Māori Loanwords: A Corpus of New Zealand English Tweets. *Proceedings of the ACL 2019 Student Research Workshop*. ACL Anthology P19-2018.** Over 30% of tweets collected by Māori-word keyword were irrelevant, because many such strings also have non-Māori meanings. The authors trained ML classifiers to filter them. Relevance: this is direct evidence of the homograph problem, also seen with place names that double as personal names. — [ACL Anthology PDF](https://preview.aclanthology.org/moar-dois/P19-2018.pdf)
- **Trye, D., Keegan, T. T., Mato, P. & Apperley, M. (2020). Hybrid Hashtags: #YouKnowYoureAKiwiWhen your Tweet contains Māori and English. *Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence*, 3:15. DOI: 10.3389/frai.2020.00015.** A diachronic corpus of over one million tweets containing Māori loanwords, used to study mixed-language hashtags and embeddings. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2020.00015/pdf). (The DOI and topic were confirmed. The author list is from my knowledge and should be checked.)
- **Trye, D., Calude, A. S., Keegan, T. T. & Falconer, J. (2022/2023). When loanwords are not lone words: Using networks and hypergraphs to explore Māori loanwords in New Zealand English. *International Journal of Corpus Linguistics*. DOI: 10.1075/ijcl.21124.try.** Māori loanwords rarely occur alone, and infrequent ones co-occur with frequent ones. NZ English is still actively borrowing from Māori. Relevance: in NZ English text, Māori names appear within dense te reo contexts, which matters for context-based NER. — [Waikato Research Commons](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/handle/10289/15465)

**Macron and diacritic restoration**
- **Cocks, J. & Keegan, T. T. (2011). A Word-based Approach for Diacritic Restoration in Māori. *Proceedings of the Australasian Language Technology Association Workshop 2011*. ACL Anthology U11-1016.** A word-level naive Bayes model using local context beats grapheme-based methods. The related thesis reports over 99% accuracy. This underpins the Waikato Māori Macron Restoration Service. Unmarked text usually replaces long vowels with short ones. — [ACL Anthology](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/U11-1016); [Waikato thesis record: "Diacritic Restoration and the Development of a Part-of-Speech Tagset for the Māori Language"](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/entities/publication/abaad391-9301-4a1f-bfdc-2214990e0463/full)
- **Georgetown MS thesis, "Using Neural Network Models for Diacritics Restoration in Māori".** Uses a 463k-word Wikipedia corpus and reaches 95.87% word accuracy with a simple neural model. — [Georgetown repository](https://repository.digital.georgetown.edu/handle/10822/1085795) **[not fetched; author not confirmed]**

**Tokenisation**
- **Petrov, A., La Malfa, E., Torr, P. H. S. & Bibi, A. (2023). Language Model Tokenizers Introduce Unfairness Between Languages. *NeurIPS 2023*.** The same text can need up to 15 times more tokens depending on the language, which affects cost, latency and how much fits in the context window. — [NeurIPS proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/74bb24dca8334adce292883b4b651eda-Abstract.html)
- **"The Token Tax: Systematic Bias in Multilingual Tokenization" (2025), arXiv:2509.05486.** Fertility (tokens per word) predicts accuracy across 10 models and 16 African languages. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.05486v1) **[not fetched; authors not confirmed]**

### Inferences
- With no te reo NER corpus available, Tūmataiti's synthetic and lexicon-based evaluation is novel. It should be framed carefully, since benchmarks drawn from Kaitiakitanga-licensed data cannot be built without agreement.
- Macron-insensitive matching is cheap. Restoration accuracy of 95–99% suggests normalising by stripping macrons for matching while keeping the original span offsets is a sound approach, and it covers the "with/without macrons" requirement.
- Shared spellings and the 30% irrelevance rate for loanword keywords both predict false positives for Māori words that are also common nouns or place names. That argues for contextual disambiguation, not pure gazetteers.
- I found no te reo-specific fertility measurement. Te reo uses a small Latin alphabet with frequent vowel sequences and macrons, so subword fragmentation is plausible, especially when macrons are written as combining characters. That hypothesis needs empirical testing, for example by comparing NFC and NFD forms.

### Gaps
- **No te reo Māori NER dataset, model or paper was found** in the ACL Anthology or arXiv searches. Absence is not proof; a targeted ACL Anthology full-text search is recommended.
- I found no peer-reviewed paper on Papa Reo ASR; its accuracy figures are from press and vendor sources.
- I found no study measuring tokeniser fertility for te reo Māori in particular.
- The brief mentions "James et al." on code-switching; that work is covered above. I found nothing by a "Calude" on code-switching specifically. Calude's work is on loanwords.

## 3. Bias in NER and PII detection across name origins, languages and varieties

### Takeaway
There is consistent evidence that English NER underperforms on names from non-majority demographic groups, from 2020 synthetic-template studies through to 2023 Global Englishes and Danish intersectionality work. In the Global Englishes dataset, **Oceania shows the largest F1 drops, over 10 F1 for some models**. No study audits Māori or Pasifika names specifically, so Tūmataiti's target of te reo name recall within 2 points of English would fill a documented gap.

### Cited Findings
- **Mishra, S., He, S. & Belli, L. (2020). Assessing Demographic Bias in Named Entity Recognition. AKBC 2020 Workshop on Bias in Automatic Knowledge Graph Construction. arXiv:2008.03415.** The authors placed names associated with different ethnicities and genders into synthetic template sentences. Models performed better on some groups' names than others, and a later survey summarises that "nonwhite names yielded on average lower performance". Debiased embeddings did not fix this. Character-based contextual models such as ELMo were least biased. Limitations: unigram names only, and older name lists. Relevance: this is the template methodology Tūmataiti's synthetic evaluation mirrors, and character-level features may help with te reo names. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03415); [KG bias survey, arXiv:2210.03353](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.03353)
- **Shan, A., Bauer, J., Carlson, R. & Manning, C. D. (2023). Do "English" Named Entity Recognizers Work Well on Global Englishes? *Findings of EMNLP 2023*. arXiv:2404.13465.** The authors built a Worldwide English NER newswire dataset. Models trained on CoNLL-03 or OntoNotes lost over 10 F1, with **the largest drops in Oceania and Africa**. A search summary of the full PDF says an "Indigenous" subset of Oceanic and Canadian native news did worst, with unfamiliar tokens leading the model to misread context; I did not verify this against the PDF. Adding the worldwide data to training cost only 1–2 F1 on the original test sets. Relevance: the closest published evidence on NZ and Pacific English. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13465)
- **"Detecting intersectionality in NER models: A data-driven approach" (2023). *Proceedings of the 7th LaTeCH-CLfL Workshop*. ACL Anthology 2023.latechclfl-1.13.** Danish NER models perform systematically worse on minority-ethnic and non-binary names than on typically Danish names. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2023.latechclfl-1.13). The fetch failed. From memory, the authors are Lassen, Almasi, Enevoldsen and Kristensen-McLachlan; check before citing.
- An African-names NER bias benchmark (NeurIPS 2025) proposes a bias index and argues that Western-centric datasets erase local names. It is a template for a Pacific equivalent. — [NeurIPS 2025 listing](https://nips.cc/virtual/2025/133796) **[not fetched; title and authors not confirmed]**

### Inferences
- Taken together, Mishra et al., Shan et al. and the Danish study predict that off-the-shelf English PII and NER models (spaCy, Presidio, transformer NER) will under-recall Māori and Pasifika names. A recall-parity target such as the 2-point gap has precedent as a fairness metric, though none of these papers sets that exact threshold.
- Shan et al. found that mixing in regional training data closes the gap at little cost to the original test sets. That supports fine-tuning or augmenting with NZ-specific synthetic data.
- Mishra et al.'s unigram-only limitation matters here. Māori names are often multi-token, and names with prefixes such as "Te" or "Ngā" create boundary errors. Tūmataiti's evaluation should report span-level as well as token-level recall.

### Gaps
- I found **no audit of NER or PII tools on Māori, Pasifika or other Polynesian names**.
- I found no study of PII detection specifically, as opposed to general NER, across name origins. A targeted search on Presidio or PII benchmarks and demographic bias is recommended.
- The per-region figures in Shan et al.'s full paper were not extracted.

## 4. Indigenous data governance in AI more broadly, and re-identification risk in small populations

### Takeaway
Internationally, the Indigenous Protocol and AI position paper (2020) and the CARE/GIDA framework set relational, community-centred expectations for AI. Te Hiku's licence has been adopted in other Polynesian projects, including Cook Islands Māori ASR. Re-identification research shows that a handful of quasi-identifiers can single out almost anyone. Iwi, hapū or marae affiliation combined with region is exactly that kind of quasi-identifier in small communities.

### Cited Findings
- **Lewis, J. E. (ed.) (2020). *Indigenous Protocol and Artificial Intelligence Position Paper*. Honolulu: Indigenous Protocol and AI Working Group; Initiative for Indigenous Futures and CIFAR.** A heterogeneous collection of guidelines, essays, art and prototypes, produced over 20 months across 20 time zones. It includes "Guidelines for Indigenous-Centred AI Design". It critiques human-centred AI ethics and favours relational and environmental framings. NZ contributors are listed by Dragonfly Data Science. — [Dragonfly listing](https://www.dragonfly.co.nz/publications/lewis_indigenous_2020/); [indigenous-ai.net](https://www.indigenous-ai.net/?p=131); [CIFAR](https://cifar.ca/?p=36479). ### Inferences
- Rocher et al. show that uniqueness rises quickly with each added attribute. In small hapū or marae communities, which may have hundreds of members, affiliation plus one or two further attributes such as town, age band or role will often be unique. This supports redacting affiliation by default, as the project brief assumes. It is an inference; I found no published NZ-specific re-identification study of iwi affiliation.
- Te Hiku's licence and the Coto-Solano model release show Kaitiakitanga-style licensing is a working precedent for releasing Tūmataiti's te reo lexicons and models under community-controlled terms, not plain open-source licences.

### Gaps
- I found no study quantifying re-identification risk from iwi, hapū or marae affiliation, or from Pacific village or church affiliation, in NZ datasets.
- Australian First Nations work (Maiam nayri Wingara principles, Lowitja Institute) and the Canadian OCAP® principles were not searched this session. They are well-known comparators, but no citations were verified here.
- Statistics NZ's confidentiality rules for iwi-level census outputs, such as cell suppression thresholds, were not retrieved. They would give an empirical basis for small-cell risk.
