# Synthetic Training/Evaluation Data and Weak Supervision for PII Detection and NER

Scope note for the report writer. Every work below was confirmed to exist. Two levels of checking apply:
- **[V-session]**: the abstract, landing page or full text was fetched or search-confirmed in this session (Nov 2026). Numbers come from those pages.
- **[V-known]**: a canonical, widely cited work whose bibliographic details are well established, given with its ACL Anthology, arXiv or DOI link. It was not re-fetched in this session, so only method-level claims are made and no specific numbers are given.

"Preprint" means arXiv-only with no peer-reviewed venue found. Project context: Tūmataiti builds NZ and te reo Māori documents from templates, lexicons and checksum-valid or invalid NZ identifiers. Its labels come from template slots, rules (used as weak labels) and an LLM teacher. It evaluates on a held-out synthetic NZ set plus ai4privacy PII-Masking-300k.

## Q1. Template-based and LLM-generated synthetic data for NER/PII: how well does it transfer, and what are the pitfalls?

### Takeaway
Synthetic and LLM-annotated data is now the default way to bootstrap PII/NER models. It works well as a **supplement** to real data and for distillation into small models (UniversalNER, NuNER, GLiNER-PII). The controlled studies agree that it does **not** fully replace real labelled text. Purely synthetic training loses some recall on real data, and gaps come from differences in length, label distribution and format. Synthetic text is also cleaner and less diverse ("John Doe" repetition, LLM style). Domain-specific clinical evidence (Dutch, veterinary, Norwegian) shows synthetic-only models reaching about 96% of identifier recall on real notes, or better on out-of-distribution formats. Substituting synthetic for real at a fixed sample size increases leakage.

### Cited Findings

**PII-specific synthetic datasets (the project's comparators)**
- **ai4privacy, PII-Masking-300k** (Hugging Face dataset, 2024; DOI 10.57967/hf/1995) [V-session]. Fully synthetic, made with "proprietary algorithms", and the generator is not disclosed. It combines OpenPII-220k (about 220k entries, about 30.4M text tokens, 7.6M PII tokens, 27 PII classes, 749 discussion subjects in education, health and psychology) with FinPII-80k (about 20 additional finance and insurance classes, gated). It covers 6 languages across 8 jurisdictions (EN UK/US, FR, DE, IT, NL, ES, including Swiss variants) with a 79/21 train/validation split. The card claims human-in-the-loop validation with about 98.3% token-label accuracy on a random sample of only 216 entries. Errors are concentrated in coarse types (country, time, titles). The licence is custom ("other"), with academic use under citation and commercial use licensed. No accompanying peer-reviewed paper exists. Relevance: the project's second test set is itself synthetic and generator-opaque, has no NZ or Māori locale, and its quality is validated on a tiny sample. — [HF dataset card README](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4privacy/pii-masking-300k/raw/main/README.md); [HF dataset page](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4privacy/pii-masking-300k)
- **Gretel, `gretelai/synthetic_pii_finance_multilingual`** (HF dataset, June 2024) [V-session, partly via secondary sources]. Apache-2.0 licence, 10K to 100K rows. Gretel's blog describes 100 financial document formats and 29 PII types in multiple languages, intended for NER training and testing de-identification. Third-party papers report about 55,940 records. One notes "limited PII diversity, with repetitive or low-quality synthetic values such as 'John Doe' or 'janedoe@mail.com'". That is a concrete artefact pitfall. — [HF repo](https://huggingface.co/datasets/gretelai/synthetic_pii_finance_multilingual/tree/3a44850c478f01b157b0ba0f02013a55920007de); [secondary summary of Gretel blog](https://plushcap.com/analysis/gretel-ai/gretel-unlocks-pii-detection-with-synthetic-financial-document-dataset); limitation quoted in [arXiv 2601.17480](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.17480) (preprint; seen only in search snippet)
- **Savkin, Ionov, Konovalov (2025). "SPY: Enhancing Privacy with Synthetic PII Detection Dataset."** NAACL 2025 Student Research Workshop, pp. 236–246. DOI 10.18653/v1/2025.naacl-srw.23 [V-session]. An LLM-generated PII dataset of 4,491 medical consultations and 4,197 legal questions. Entities are stored as **placeholders filled at load time with Faker** under a seed, so the same text skeleton can carry varied surrogate values. That is close to Tūmataiti's template-slot design. The authors find standard NER models underperform on PII-specific detection. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-srw.23); [HF dataset mks-logic/SPY](https://huggingface.co/datasets/mks-logic/SPY)
- **NVIDIA, Nemotron-PII and GLiNER-PII** (HF blog and dataset, 2025) [V-session]. 100K synthetic records (50k train, 50k test) with more than 55 PII types across more than 50 industries, in structured and unstructured formats. Built with NeMo Data Designer: templates grounded in real field distributions, LLM free-text augmentation (e.g. Mistral-Small-24B), and **persona grounding** via Nemotron-Personas, which reflect demographic and geographic distributions. CC BY 4.0. It reports 92% recall and 64% F1 for GLiNER-PII, but the blog does not name the test set or baselines. This is vendor-reported and unverified. — [HF blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/nvidia/nemotron-pii)
- **GLiNER2-PII** (arXiv 2605.09973, 2026, preprint) [V-session, via aggregator only]. Reportedly trained entirely on synthetic data for 42 PII types across 7 languages. The authors flag real-world evaluation as future work. Verify against the original before citing. — [EmergentMind summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2605.09973)
- **Microsoft Presidio-research data generator** (GitHub tooling) [V-known]. A template plus Faker generator for synthetic PII sentences used to evaluate Presidio. This is industry precedent for template-slot labelling. — [GitHub microsoft/presidio-research](https://github.com/microsoft/presidio-research)

**Synthetic clinical notes for de-identification (transfer evidence)**
- **Libbi, Trienes, Trieschnigg, Seifert (2021). "Generating Synthetic Training Data for Supervised De-Identification of Electronic Health Records."** Future Internet 13(5):136. DOI 10.3390/fi13050136 [V-session]. LSTM and GPT-2 generate artificial Dutch EHR text, with annotations from a pre-trained de-id tool ("deidentify"). Models trained on synthetic documents beat a rule-based baseline, and real plus synthetic improved recall. A user study flags privacy risk from LMs copying rare real chunks. — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/1999-5903/13/5/136/htm); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/a07111ba25da4ce4a09c1cbfdcceb0e7)
- **Lund, Burman, Woldaregay, Jenssen, Mikalsen (2024). "Instruction-guided deidentification with synthetic test cases for Norwegian clinical text."** Proceedings of the 5th Northern Lights Deep Learning Conference (NLDL), PMLR vol. 233 [V-session, full text read]. GPT-4 generated 1,200 synthetic Norwegian discharge summaries (about 100 words each) with PHI annotations. Generated annotations agreed with human annotation at **F1 = 0.983**. GPT-4 prompted to annotate or redact **underperformed** the Bråthen et al. NER baseline on NorSynthClinical-PHI, though it did better on its own synthetic set (redaction F1 0.859 vs baseline). The authors flag a possible bias in GPT-4's favour on GPT-4-generated test data. This is an example of the self-preference risk in synthetic evaluation. — [PMLR PDF](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v233/lund24a/lund24a.pdf)
- **Hellemans, Stroobants, Scheurwegs, Meysman, Jorens, Laukens (2026). "MedDeID enables locally governed clinical-text de-identification from real or synthetic training data."** arXiv 2609.10049, Sep 2026 (preprint) [V-session]. An on-premises framework with a compact transformer. On 300 real Dutch hospital notes, the hospital-trained model reached 98.9% identifier detection and the **synthetic-only model 96.1%**. On 100 real primary-care notes, the **synthetic-trained model had higher recall (90.3% vs 87.0%)** and was more robust to identifier-format perturbations. English synthetic-only results (99.7% and 98.9%) were on synthetic benchmarks only. The authors state explicitly that this does not show clinical-English performance. Highly relevant: it is a local or on-prem design, and the format-perturbation robustness parallels Tūmataiti's valid and invalid checksum variants. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10049)
- **Brundage (2026). "Synthetic Data for Veterinary EHR De-identification: Benefits, Limits, and Safety Trade-offs Under Fixed Compute."** arXiv 2601.09756 (preprint) [V-session]. Built on PetEVAL (1,249 train and 3,750 holdout documents) with 10,382 LLM-generated notes, using template-only prompting with identifiers removed. **Substituting synthetic for real at fixed sample size steadily increased document-level leakage.** Epoch-scaled augmentation raised PetBERT span F1 from 0.831 to 0.850 and cut leakage from 6.32% to 4.02%. The author attributes the gains mainly to extra training exposure, not synthetic quality. Synthetic and real notes differed systematically in **length and label distribution**. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09756)
- **Sarkar, Chuang, Mohammed, Jiang (2024).** arXiv 2402.00179 (preprint as found; exact title and published venue not confirmed in session) [V-session, snippet only]. Compares de-identified with LLM-generated clinical notes. Synthetic notes that match real-data utility also show similar privacy (membership-inference) exposure, and de-identifying real notes did not stop membership inference. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.00179v2)
- **Liu, Huang, Yu, et al. (2023). "DeID-GPT: Zero-shot Medical Text De-Identification by GPT-4."** arXiv 2303.11032 (preprint) [V-known]. GPT-4 prompted for i2b2/n2c2 2014 de-id. Lund et al. cite it as reporting better recall than a fine-tuned ClinicalBERT. Relevant to using an LLM as teacher. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11032)
- **Bråthen, Wie, Dalianis (2021). "Creating and Evaluating a Synthetic Norwegian Clinical Corpus for De-Identification."** NoDaLiDa 2021 [V-known; cited as the baseline in Lund et al.]. NorSynthClinical-PHI: a manually written synthetic clinical corpus used as a public de-id benchmark. — [ACL Anthology search](https://aclanthology.org/2021.nodalida-main.25)

### Inferences
- The best-supported recipe is **real-or-realistic text plus synthetic augmentation**, not synthetic alone. Without consented real NZ text, Tūmataiti should expect a recall gap on real documents, of roughly the 2–3 points seen in MedDeID's hospital setting and possibly more for genre shift.
- Template-slot labels (SPY with Faker, Presidio, Nemotron) give perfect span alignment but inherit **template artefacts**: fixed contexts, clean formatting, repetitive values. LLM-generated text adds fluency but brings LLM style and label noise (ProgGen, ZeroGen). Attribute-diversified prompting (Yu et al. 2023; Nemotron persona grounding) is the documented mitigation.
- Checksum-invalid identifiers act as **format perturbations**. MedDeID's finding that synthetic training improves robustness to identifier-format perturbations supports deliberately including near-miss formats.

### Gaps
- I found no published study evaluating ai4privacy-trained models on real, human-annotated PII text. Ai4privacy publishes no paper and does not disclose its generator.
- I found no NZ, Australian or te reo Māori synthetic PII dataset or study.
- I could not verify the full Gretel dataset card text or Nemotron-PII's evaluation protocol. GLiNER2-PII was seen only via an aggregator.

## Q2. Surrogate (realistic replacement) generation for de-identified corpora

### Takeaway
"Hiding in plain sight" (HIPS) shows that replacing detected PII with realistic surrogates makes residual leaked identifiers hard to find. Human readers found only about 9–13% of leaks in the 2012 pilot and about 27% recall in a 2020 adversarial study, so about 70% stayed hidden. Surrogate generation is hard: consistency, format, gender and culture all matter. That is the same problem as Tūmataiti's lexicon and identifier generation, and the clinical de-id benchmarks (i2b2/n2c2) are themselves surrogate-filled.

### Cited Findings
- **Carrell, Malin, Aberdeen, Bayer, Clark, Wellner, Hirschman (2013). "Hiding in plain sight: use of realistic surrogates to reduce exposure of protected health information in clinical text."** JAMIA 20(2):342–348 (online July 2012). DOI 10.1136/amiajnl-2012-001034 [V-session]. A pilot with realistic surrogates replacing detected PHI. Reviewers found **6/47 residual identifiers (recall 0.13)** in a high-density oncology corpus and **6/66 (recall 0.09)** in family-practice notes, so about 87–91% stayed undetected. They project that systems with 94–98% recall could exceed 99% effective protection. This is a projection, not a test, from a small pilot. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3638183); [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/jamia/article/20/2/342/897812)
- **Carrell, Malin, Cronkite, Aberdeen, Clark, Li, Bastakoty, Nyemba, Hirschman (2020). "Resilience of clinical text de-identified with 'hiding in plain sight' to hostile reidentification attacks by human readers."** JAMIA 27(9):1374–1382. DOI 10.1093/jamia/ocaa095 [V-session]. Two 100-document corpora (KP Washington, VUMC) and four readers. Mean recall of leaked PII was **26.8%** and precision 37.2%. By type: organisations 45%, dates 32%, doctor names 25%, patient names 23%, ages 9%. Insiders reached 32% vs outsiders 22%. About **70% of leaked PII stayed undetected**, which is less than against an ML-assisted attack. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7647331)
- A 2019 JAMIA study of a machine "parrot attack" on HIPS corpora (DOI 10.1093/jamia/ocz114) is referenced by the 2020 paper. Its title was not verified in session. — [Crossref listing](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1093%2FJAMIA%2FOCZ114)
- **Stubbs & Uzuner (2015). "Annotating longitudinal clinical narratives for de-identification: The 2014 i2b2/UTHealth corpus."** J Biomed Inform 58(Suppl):S20–S29. DOI 10.1016/j.jbi.2015.07.020 [V-known]. The standard de-id benchmark, in which PHI was replaced with realistic surrogates. Most clinical de-id "real" evaluations are therefore partly synthetic at the entity level. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbi.2015.07.020)
- **Stubbs, Uzuner, Kotfila, Goldstein, Szolovits (2015). "Challenges in Synthesizing Surrogate PHI in Narrative EMRs."** In Gkoulalas-Divanis & Loukides (eds.), *Medical Data Privacy Handbook*, Springer, pp. 717–735. DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-23633-9_27 [V-known]. Catalogues surrogate pitfalls: consistency across a record, temporal shifting of dates, preserving format and gender or ethnicity cues, and ambiguous or misspelled PHI. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-23633-9_27)
- **Uzuner, Luo, Szolovits (2007). "Evaluating the state-of-the-art in automatic de-identification."** JAMIA 14(5):550–563. DOI 10.1197/jamia.M2444 [V-known]. The 2006 i2b2 challenge, whose corpus used surrogates, including deliberately ambiguous ones. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1197/jamia.M2444)
- **Eder, Krieg-Holz, Hahn (2020). "CodE Alltag 2.0 — A Pseudonymized German-Language Email Corpus."** LREC 2020 [V-known]. Builds on **Eder et al. (2019). "De-Identification of Emails: Pseudonymizing Privacy-Sensitive Data in a German Email Corpus."** RANLP 2019. Type-preserving pseudonym substitution for non-clinical text. — [ACL Anthology LREC 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.552); [RANLP 2019](https://aclanthology.org/R19-1030)
- **Yermilov, Raheja, Ilinykh (2023). "Privacy- and Utility-Preserving NLP with Anonymized Data: A Case Study of Pseudonymization."** TrustNLP 2023. arXiv 2306.05561 [V-known]. Compares NER-based and LLM-based pseudonymisation and measures downstream utility of models trained on pseudonymised text. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05561)
- **Pilán, Lison, Øvrelid, Papadopoulou, Sánchez, Batet (2022). "The Text Anonymization Benchmark (TAB): A Dedicated Corpus and Evaluation Framework for Text Anonymization."** Computational Linguistics 48(4). arXiv 2202.00443. DOI 10.1162/coli_a_00458 [V-known]. A real (ECHR court cases), human-annotated benchmark with privacy-oriented recall metrics weighted by direct vs quasi-identifiers. The main non-synthetic, non-clinical counterpoint for evaluation design. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.00443)

### Inferences
- Tūmataiti's lexicon plus identifier generator is effectively a **surrogate generator**. It can do double duty: training-data synthesis, and HIPS-style replacement at redaction time instead of [MASK] tokens. HIPS evidence suggests the latter reduces the harm of missed spans.
- The surrogate literature's consistency requirements (same person keeps the same surrogate, gender and culture match, format preserved) should also apply to the synthetic generator to avoid detectable artefacts.

### Gaps
- No surrogate-generation work was found that addresses Māori or Pacific names, iwi or hapū affiliations, or macron handling.

## Q3. Weak supervision for NER: labelling functions, HMM aggregation, self-training and teacher pseudo-labelling

### Takeaway
A mature line of work (Snorkel, Lison et al. 2020 / skweak, linked HMMs, CHMM, WRENCH) shows that combining several noisy labellers (rules, gazetteers, off-the-shelf models) with a sequence-aware generative aggregator (HMM) beats majority voting and single sources. A neural student trained on the aggregated labels generalises beyond the rules. LLM teachers (GPT-3, ChatGPT, GPT-4) now act as an especially strong labelling function. Noise-robust self-training (BOND, Meng et al.) is the standard way to refine pseudo-labels.

### Cited Findings
- **Ratner, De Sa, Wu, Selsam, Ré (2016). "Data Programming: Creating Large Training Sets, Quickly."** NeurIPS 2016. arXiv 1605.07723 [V-known]. A generative model over labelling-function votes that learns accuracies without ground truth. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.07723)
- **Ratner, Bach, Ehrenberg, Fries, Wu, Ré (2017). "Snorkel: Rapid Training Data Creation with Weak Supervision."** PVLDB 11(3) (extended in VLDB Journal 2020). arXiv 1711.10160 [V-known]. A system for writing labelling functions and training discriminative end models on denoised labels. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.10160)
- **Lison, Barnes, Hubin, Touileb (2020). "Named Entity Recognition without Labelled Data: A Weak Supervision Approach."** ACL 2020. DOI 10.18653/v1/2020.acl-main.139. arXiv 2004.14723 [V-known]. Many labelling functions (gazetteers, heuristics, document-level constraints, out-of-domain NER models) are aggregated with a **multi-source HMM** whose emission parameters capture each source's confusion. A neural NER is then trained on the aggregate. It substantially outperforms majority voting and out-of-domain models on news-domain targets. The closest methodological match to Tūmataiti's "rules as weak labels". — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.139); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14723)
- **Lison, Barnes, Hubin (2021). "skweak: Weak Supervision Made Easy for NLP."** ACL-IJCNLP 2021 System Demonstrations. DOI 10.18653/v1/2021.acl-demo.40. arXiv 2104.09683 [V-known]. A spaCy-based Python toolkit for labelling functions plus HMM or majority-vote aggregation for sequence labelling. Directly usable with Python-side rule outputs. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-demo.40); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09683)
- **Safranchik, Luo, Bach (2020). "Weakly Supervised Sequence Tagging from Noisy Rules."** AAAI 2020. DOI 10.1609/aaai.v34i04.6009 [V-known]. A **linked HMM** with "tagging rules" plus "linking rules" (whether adjacent tokens share a tag). Useful for multi-token identifiers and addresses. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v34i04.6009)
- **Lan, Huang, Lin, Liu, Ren (2020). "Learning to Contextually Aggregate Multi-Source Supervision for Sequence Labeling."** ACL 2020. arXiv 1910.04289 [V-known]. CONNET learns source reliabilities conditioned on context. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.04289)
- **Li, Shetty, Liu, Zhang, Song (2021). "BERTifying the Hidden Markov Model for Multi-Source Weakly Supervised Named Entity Recognition."** ACL 2021. arXiv 2105.12848 [V-known]. CHMM: BERT embeddings parameterise HMM transitions and emissions per token, improving over plain HMM aggregation. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.12848)
- **Zhang, Yu, Li, Wang, Yang, Yang, Ratner (2021). "WRENCH: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Weak Supervision."** NeurIPS 2021 Datasets & Benchmarks. arXiv 2109.11377 [V-known]. Includes sequence-tagging weak-supervision datasets and compares label models (HMM, CHMM, majority vote) with end models. A reference for evaluating aggregators. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.11377)
- **Shang, Liu, Jiang, Ren, Voss, Han (2018). "Learning Named Entity Tagger using Domain-Specific Dictionary."** EMNLP 2018 (AutoNER). DOI 10.18653/v1/D18-1230 [V-known]. Dictionary-based distant supervision with a "Tie or Break" scheme that handles incomplete dictionary matches. Relevant to lexicon-matching labellers (NZ place and name lexicons). — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/D18-1230)
- **Liang, Yu, Jiang, Er, Wang, Zhao, Zhang (2020). "BOND: BERT-Assisted Open-Domain Named Entity Recognition with Distant Supervision."** KDD 2020. arXiv 2006.15509 [V-known]. Stage 1 fine-tunes a PLM on distant labels with early stopping. Stage 2 runs teacher-student self-training with soft pseudo-labels. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.15509)
- **Meng, Zhang, Huang, Wang, Zhang, Ji, Han (2021). "Distantly-Supervised Named Entity Recognition with Noise-Robust Learning and Language Model Augmented Self-Training."** EMNLP 2021. arXiv 2109.05003 [V-known]. A noise-robust loss plus removal of unreliable labels, then self-training with LM-augmented contexts. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05003)
- **Xie, Luong, Hovy, Le (2020). "Self-training with Noisy Student improves ImageNet classification."** CVPR 2020. arXiv 1911.04252 [V-known]. The canonical teacher, then noised student, then iterate recipe adopted in NLP pseudo-labelling. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.04252)
- **Huang, Li, Ma, et al. (2021). "Few-Shot Named Entity Recognition: An Empirical Baseline Study."** EMNLP 2021. arXiv 2012.14978 [V-known]. Finds that noisy supervised pre-training and self-training on unlabelled in-domain text both consistently help few-shot NER. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.14978)
- LLM-as-teacher in this framing: Wang et al. 2021 (GPT-3 labels), UniversalNER, NuNER and DeID-GPT (all in Q1) treat an LLM as a high-coverage labelling source whose output is distilled into a small model.

### Inferences
- Tūmataiti has three label sources: template slots (gold on synthetic), deterministic rules (high precision, low recall, narrow types) and an LLM teacher (broad coverage, hallucinated or mis-bounded spans). This maps cleanly to the Lison et al. / skweak setup. Rather than taking a union or overriding by priority, an HMM or CHMM aggregator over rules plus LLM spans can learn per-source confusion. On synthetic data, template-slot gold can be used to **estimate each source's accuracy** before applying the aggregator to unlabelled real-style text.
- Checksum validation is a strong precision signal. It can act as a high-confidence labelling function and as a filter on teacher pseudo-labels.
- Mitigate model-collapse risk in multi-round self-training by keeping the rules and template gold fixed as anchors and capping the number of iterations (Shumailov et al. 2024).

### Gaps
- No weak-supervision study specifically on PII (as opposed to general NER or clinical de-id) was found in this pass.
- I did not find head-to-head comparisons of LLM-teacher pseudo-labels against HMM-aggregated rule labels for PII.

## Q4. Validity of synthetic test sets for evaluation

### Takeaway
Recent evidence warns that LLM-generated benchmarks can be valid yet **easier** than human-made data. They track model rankings for simple tasks but **not reliably for NER**, and they can favour the generating model (self-preference). Synthetic test sets are defensible as *diagnostic* or *stress* suites (format robustness, name-diversity audits, checksum near-misses). Headline claims about real-world performance need at least a small real or consented, human-annotated set. Note also that the standard "real" clinical benchmarks are surrogate-filled.

### Cited Findings
- **Maheshwari, Ivanov, El Haddad (2024). "Efficacy of Synthetic Data as a Benchmark."** arXiv 2409.11968 (preprint) [V-session]. Covers three tasks (intent detection, text similarity, NER) over six datasets. Synthetic benchmarks mirrored model performance well for intent classification but **"significantly less so for more complex tasks like NER"**. They introduce a "bias factor" for when the same LLM generates the data and performs the task. Smaller LLMs prefer their own data and larger ones do not. They recommend data from multiple larger models. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11968v1); [Diabolocom summary](https://www.diabolocom.com/research/synthetic-text-data-evaluating-llm-generated-benchmarks-nlp-tasks/)
- **Gill, Ravichander, Marasović (2025). "What Has Been Lost with Synthetic Evaluation?"** Findings of EMNLP 2025. arXiv 2505.22830 [V-session]. LLM-generated versions of CondaQA and DROP were often valid under the original guidelines and cheaper, but were **easier** for models than human-authored originals. LLMs-as-benchmark-creators are "not advisable as of yet", especially if validity is the only check. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22830); [ACL Anthology preview](https://preview.aclanthology.org/dashboard/2025.findings-emnlp.526)
- Lund et al. (2024) flag possible GPT-4 bias on GPT-4-generated test notes. GPT-4's ranking relative to the baseline **flipped** between their synthetic set and NorSynthClinical-PHI. This is concrete evidence that synthetic test sets can change conclusions. — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v233/lund24a/lund24a.pdf)
- MedDeID (2026) explicitly declines to claim clinical-English performance from synthetic-only English benchmarks despite 98.9–99.7% detection. This is the best-practice phrasing to emulate. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10049)
- Brundage (2026): systematic length and label-distribution differences between synthetic and real notes explain persistent leakage. Report distributional diagnostics alongside synthetic scores. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09756)
- Privacy-oriented evaluation: TAB (Pilán et al. 2022) argues for recall-weighted metrics that distinguish direct from quasi-identifiers and count entity-level (all mentions) masking. Carrell et al. (2020) show that leak types differ in re-identifiability. — [TAB arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.00443); [Carrell 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7647331)
- Synthetic vs real privacy: Sarkar et al. (2024) find that synthetic notes matching real-data utility carry similar membership-inference risk. Libbi et al. (2021) note LMs can copy rare real chunks. So "synthetic" does not automatically mean "safe to publish" when the generator was fine-tuned on real data. — [arXiv 2402.00179](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.00179v2); [Libbi et al.](https://www.mdpi.com/1999-5903/13/5/136/htm)

### Inferences
- Tūmataiti's held-out synthetic NZ set measures **in-distribution fit to its own generator**. If the same templates and lexicons produce train and test, scores are optimistic. Hold out whole templates, document genres and lexicon partitions (unseen names and places), not just random documents.
- PII-Masking-300k is not an independent "real" check. It is another synthetic generator with different artefacts. Its main value is as a **cross-generator** test, and it lacks NZ or Māori entities. Report both, but label both as synthetic.
- Best practice synthesised from the above: (1) a small consented or public real NZ set with human annotation for headline numbers (e.g. public-domain NZ government documents with synthetic PII inserted are a middle ground); (2) generator-disjoint synthetic tests; (3) avoid using the teacher LLM to generate the test set it is later scored on; (4) report distribution diagnostics and per-type recall plus document-level leakage.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that specifically assesses the validity of synthetic PII test sets, or that compares synthetic with consented real data for PII redaction outside clinical settings.

## Q5. Data augmentation for NER (entity/mention replacement) and name diversity / fairness

### Takeaway
Simple mention replacement (swap an entity for another of the same type) is among the most effective NER augmentations in low-resource settings. The same operation, entity switching by national or ethnic origin, is the standard method for auditing NER and PII bias. PII maskers show significant error-rate disparities, worst for names associated with Black and Asian/Pacific Islander individuals, and NER performance varies with entity origin. No study covers Māori names, so a Tūmataiti audit would be novel.

### Cited Findings
- **Dai & Adel (2020). "An Analysis of Simple Data Augmentation for Named Entity Recognition."** COLING 2020. arXiv 2010.11683 [V-known]. Compares label-wise token replacement, synonym replacement, **mention replacement** and shuffle-within-segments on biomedical and materials NER. Simple augmentation helps most in small-data regimes. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11683)
- **Ding, Liu, Bing, Kruengkrai, Nguyen, Joty, Si, Miao (2020). "DAGA: Data Augmentation with a Generation Approach for Low-resource Tagging Tasks."** EMNLP 2020. DOI 10.18653/v1/2020.emnlp-main.488 [V-known]. An LM trained on linearised labelled sentences generates new tagged data. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2020.emnlp-main.488)
- **Zhou, Li, He, Bing, Cambria, Si, Miao (2022). "MELM: Data Augmentation with Masked Entity Language Modeling for Low-Resource NER."** ACL 2022. arXiv 2108.13655 [V-known]. Masks entity tokens and lets a label-conditioned MLM fill new entities. This increases entity diversity while keeping context. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13655)
- **Lin, Gao, Yan, Moreno, Ren (2021). "RockNER: A Simple Method to Create Adversarial Examples for Evaluating the Robustness of Named Entity Recognition Models."** EMNLP 2021. arXiv 2109.05620 [V-known]. Entity-level attacks replace entities with same-type Wikidata entities, plus context-level perturbations. Models rely heavily on memorised entity names. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05620)
- **Agarwal, Yang, Wallace, Nenkova (2020/2021). "Entity-Switched Datasets: An Approach to Auditing the In-Domain Robustness of Named Entity Recognition Models."** arXiv 2004.04123 (v2 Jan 2021; preprint as found) [V-session]. Replaces entities with same-type entities of different national origin. Systems do best on American and Indian entities and worst on Vietnamese and Indonesian. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04123); [code](https://github.com/oagarwal/entity-switched-ner)
- **Mishra, He, Belli (2020). "Assessing Demographic Bias in Named Entity Recognition."** arXiv 2008.03415 (presented at the AKBC 2020 Bias in Automatic Knowledge Graph Construction workshop) [V-known]. Template sentences filled with names from different ethnicity and gender groups reveal systematic recall differences in NER models and embeddings. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03415)
- **Mansfield, Paullada, Howell (2022). "Behind the Mask: Demographic bias in name detection for PII masking."** LT-EDI 2022 (ACL workshop). DOI 10.18653/v1/2022.ltedi-1.10. arXiv 2205.04505 [V-session]. Three off-the-shelf PII maskers were tested on **synthetic template plus name data** in customer service. All showed significant demographic error-rate gaps, highest for names associated with Black and Asian/Pacific Islander individuals. The open-source RoBERTa system had smaller gaps than the commercial ones. The authors argue that failing to mask marginalised groups' data causes disproportionate harm. Most directly relevant to Māori and Pasifika names in NZ text. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2022.ltedi-1.10); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.04505); [code](https://github.com/csmansfield/pii-masking-bias)
- Yu et al. (2023) (Q1) document regional and cultural bias in LLM-generated training data, which is relevant if the teacher LLM under-generates Māori names. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15895)

### Inferences
- Tūmataiti's lexicon-driven generation is mention replacement at scale. The same machinery gives a **counterfactual fairness audit**: identical templates filled with Māori, Pasifika, Pākehā/European, Asian and other names (and macron vs no-macron spellings), reporting per-group recall following Mansfield et al. (2022) and Agarwal et al. (2020).
- Māori names that are also common nouns or place names (e.g. words for natural features) are likely to be a specific failure mode, analogous to the ambiguity findings in RockNER and Entity-Switched. This is untested and a gap.

### Gaps
- No published NER or PII bias study covering Māori, Pasifika or other NZ names was found.
- No study was found measuring the effect of macron normalisation on name detection.
